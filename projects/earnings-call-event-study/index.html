Earnings Call, Surprises /w Market Reactions

June 28, 2026

An empirical event-study project examining how quarterly earnings surprises and earnings-call characteristics are associated with short-window abnormal stock returns.

The project combines earnings metadata, daily market data and earnings-call transcripts for a panel of large U.S. companies over four quarterly earnings seasons in 2025.

Overview

Earnings announcements provide a natural setting for studying how new information is incorporated into stock prices.

This project investigates two related questions:

  1. Do earnings surprises correspond to abnormal stock returns around the earnings announcement?
  2. Does information contained in the accompanying earnings-call transcript provide additional explanatory information about the market reaction?

The analysis combines conventional event-study methods with transcript-level sentiment measures and TF-IDF/SVD text representations.

The workflow is organized as a reproducible sequence from data collection through final statistical analysis.

Event Study

The event-study component uses daily stock returns together with the S&P 500 benchmark.

Abnormal returns are accumulated around each earnings announcement to produce several event-window cumulative abnormal returns (CARs).

The main windows are:

  • [-1, +1]
  • [-2, +2]
  • [0, +1]
  • [-5, +5]

The multiple windows allow the analysis to compare the immediate announcement response with a wider response surrounding the earnings event.

Earnings Surprise

The primary earnings-information variable is the reported EPS surprise percentage.

The raw distribution is highly skewed because some earnings estimates are close to zero. The sample contains several extreme observations, including unusually large positive and negative reported surprises.

To reduce the influence of this scale effect, the analysis uses the signed logarithmic transformation

$$ x^* = \operatorname{sign}(x)\log(1+|x|). $$

The transformed variable preserves the direction of the surprise while compressing extreme magnitudes.

For example, the largest raw surprises become much more manageable on the transformed scale:

ObservationRaw surprise (%)Transformed
INTC 2025Q3-1169.52-7.065

INTC 2025Q22769.767.927

The raw EPS-surprise variable has a mean of 20.77% and a standard deviation of 222.35%, while the transformed variable has a mean of 1.52 and a standard deviation of 1.72.

Transcript Features

Structural features

  • management word count

Sentiment features

  • mean sentiment
  • sentiment standard deviation
  • minimum sentiment
  • maximum sentiment

Across the final sample, the average transcript contains approximately 8,170 words, with approximately 15.5 analyst entries and 25.1 management entries.

Mean transcript sentiment is approximately 0.285.

Economic Magnitude

The fixed-effects EPS coefficients provide a useful measure of economic magnitude.

A one-unit increase in the transformed EPS surprise corresponds to the following fitted change in CAR:

CAR windowImplied CAR change
[-1,+1]1.27%
[-2,+2]1.44%
[0,+1]1.15%

For example, in the [-1,+1] window, increasing the signed log EPS surprise by one unit is associated with approximately 1.27 percentage points higher CAR, holding the company effect constant.

These are regression-based associations and should not be interpreted as causal effects.

Main Findings

The analysis produces three main conclusions.

1. EPS surprise is the strongest and most robust predictor

The transformed EPS surprise has a positive relationship with CAR across every event window.

The relationship remains statistically significant after controlling for company fixed effects.

2. Sentiment has a pooled association but not a robust within-company association

Transcript sentiment is positively associated with CAR in pooled regressions.

After company fixed effects are introduced, the sentiment coefficients become statistically insignificant.

This suggests that persistent differences in communication style across firms may account for much of the pooled sentiment relationship.

3. Transcript language contains strong industry structure

TF-IDF and SVD reveal clear thematic differences across earnings calls.

The resulting components capture recognizable business domains such as pharmaceuticals, banking, technology, consumer products, defense, telecommunications and tobacco.

However, the current analysis does not establish a broad independent predictive effect of transcript content on abnormal returns.

Limitations

Sample size

The final sample contains 395 events across 99 companies. This is useful for an event-study panel but relatively small for high-dimensional text modelling.

EPS surprise near zero

Percentage EPS surprises can become extremely large when the analyst estimate is close to zero. The signed logarithmic transformation reduces, but does not eliminate, the effect of these observations.

Text representation

TF-IDF is a lexical representation. It captures distinctive words and phrases but does not fully capture context, semantics or the meaning of statements.

Multiple comparisons

The SVD analysis examines multiple textual components across multiple event windows. Individual significant coefficients should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Causal interpretation

The regression results identify statistical associations. They do not by themselves establish that EPS surprises or transcript characteristics cause the observed stock-price movements.

Final Remark

The central empirical result is straightforward: earnings surprises are consistently associated with short-window abnormal stock returns, and this relationship remains strong after controlling for company-specific effects.

Transcript sentiment and textual content provide additional information about the structure of earnings calls, but their relationship with market reactions is substantially less robust.

The results therefore suggest that, in this sample, the numerical surprise in reported earnings carries a more stable market signal than broad measures of earnings-call sentiment or transcript-level textual content.
