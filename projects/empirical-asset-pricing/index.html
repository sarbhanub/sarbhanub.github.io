Asset Pricing: CAPM vs. FF3 vs. FF5

July 12, 2026

Question: Which asset-pricing model best explains the returns of the 25 Size × Book-to-Market portfolios: CAPM, Fama-French 3-factor, or Fama-French 5-factor?

This project evaluates the three models using monthly time-series regressions on 25 value-weighted Size × Book-to-Market portfolios over a common sample from July 1963 through June 2026.

The analysis evaluates model performance from several complementary perspectives:

  1. Pricing errors: How large are the estimated portfolio alphas?
  2. Explanatory power: How much time-series variation in returns does each model explain?
  3. Residual risk: How much unexplained volatility remains?
  4. Multiple testing: Which individual alphas remain statistically significant after Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction?
  5. Joint pricing: Does the model simultaneously explain all 25 portfolios according to the GRS test?

Key Finding

The major improvement occurs when moving from CAPM to FF3. FF5 improves statistical fit only marginally beyond FF3, and none of the three models eliminates the joint pricing errors detected by the GRS test.

What the comparison shows

  • CAPM is dominated by FF3. Mean absolute alpha falls from 0.198% to 0.087%, mean $R^2$ rises from 73.42% to 91.00%, and mean residual volatility falls from 2.94% to 1.66%.
  • FF3 captures almost all of the improvement beyond CAPM. Moving from FF3 to FF5 increases mean $R^2$ by only 0.64 percentage points and reduces mean residual volatility by only 0.06 percentage points.
  • FF5 wins on some global fit measures, but not uniformly on pricing performance. It has the highest $R^2$, lowest residual volatility, and lowest GRS statistic, yet mean absolute alpha remains essentially unchanged from FF3.
  • FF5 is selective rather than broadly superior. FF3 produces the smallest absolute alpha for 13 of 25 portfolios, compared with 9 for FF5. RMW and CMA improve some portfolios without delivering a uniform reduction in pricing errors.
  • No model passes the joint GRS test. The GRS statistic declines monotonically from 4.170 → 3.610 → 3.227, but the joint zero-alpha restriction remains rejected for all three models.

Bottom line: FF3 is the pragmatic, parsimonious choice for this portfolio set. FF5 is a useful expanded benchmark, but its incremental value is small and concentrated rather than broad-based.

Sample Design

The FF3 factors and the 25 Size × Book-to-Market portfolios begin in July 1926, while the FF5 factors begin in July 1963 because the RMW and CMA factors are only available from CRSP from that point onward.

This creates two distinct sample concepts:

  • Independent model estimation: each model retains the full sample available to it.
  • Head-to-head comparison: CAPM, FF3, and FF5 are compared over the identical July 1963 – June 2026 window containing 756 monthly observations.

The common-window design avoids attributing differences between models to differences in sample periods.

Pipeline

01 — Acquisition

The acquisition stage downloads the raw ZIP archives into an immutable data/raw/ directory using skip-if-exists behavior. The pipeline then:

  • validates archive integrity;
  • extracts the underlying files;
  • isolates the correct monthly data section;
  • selects the value-weighted portfolio returns where applicable;
  • parses YYYYMM dates into month-start timestamps;
  • validates chronological ordering;
  • checks for duplicate dates;
  • checks French missing-value codes (-99.99 / -999);
  • verifies complete monthly continuity.

Validation result: zero missing months, zero duplicate dates, and zero French missing-value codes across all three datasets.

02 — Processing

Notebook 02 re-parses directly from the raw files rather than depending on Notebook 01’s runtime state.

The processing stage:

  1. Standardizes dates to month-start timestamps.
  2. Converts returns from percentage points to decimal form (/100).
  3. Constructs portfolio excess returns as $R_{i,t}-R_{f,t}$ using the FF3 risk-free rate.
  4. Constructs the common FF3–FF5–portfolio sample covering 1963-07 – 2026-06 (756 months) for all head-to-head model comparisons in Notebook 07.
  5. Retains the full 1926–2026 datasets for models estimated independently.
  6. Compares FF3 RF and FF5 RF over the overlapping sample.
  7. Verifies that the maximum absolute difference between the two risk-free series is 0.0, validating the use of the FF3-based excess-return series for FF5 regressions.
  8. Persists processed datasets to disk.
  9. Reloads the persisted datasets and verifies their expected shapes.

Methodology

The project evaluates the models at both the individual-portfolio and joint-portfolio levels.

Test Assets

The test assets are the 25 value-weighted portfolios formed on Size × Book-to-Market.

The portfolios can be viewed as a 5 × 5 grid:

  • moving from smaller to larger portfolios changes the size dimension;
  • moving from low to high Book-to-Market changes the value dimension.

This structure makes the portfolios useful for testing whether the market factor alone, or additional size/value/profitability/investment factors, explain systematic differences in returns.

Time-Series Regressions

For each model, a separate time-series regression is estimated for each of the 25 portfolios.

OLS is used with Newey-West HAC standard errors using 12 lags throughout the pricing analysis.

The central quantity is the intercept, or alpha.

A zero alpha means that the factor model accounts for the portfolio’s expected excess return within the regression framework.

A non-zero alpha represents a remaining pricing error.

Pricing Errors

The primary descriptive measures of pricing error are:

  • mean alpha;
  • median alpha;
  • mean absolute alpha;
  • maximum absolute alpha.

Mean absolute alpha is especially useful for cross-model comparison because positive and negative pricing errors cannot cancel one another.

Explanatory Power

The time-series $R^2$ measures how much of the variation in portfolio excess returns is explained by the factors.

A high $R^2$ should not be interpreted as evidence that a model prices the assets successfully.

A model can explain a large share of return variation while still leaving statistically significant pricing errors.

This distinction is central to the interpretation of the results.

Benjamini-Hochberg FDR Correction

Twenty-five portfolio-level alpha hypotheses are tested within each factor model.

For the individual alpha tests, Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction is applied at the 5% level.

This controls the expected proportion of false discoveries among the rejected hypotheses and prevents the portfolio-level significance analysis from treating 25 separate tests as though only one test had been performed.

The results are therefore reported in both forms:

  • raw significance at the 5% level;
  • BH-adjusted significance at the 5% level.

GRS Joint Test

The central joint pricing restriction is:

$$H_0:\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\cdots=\alpha_{25}=0$$

The Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) test asks whether all 25 portfolio alphas are jointly consistent with zero.

This is a stronger requirement than examining the individual alpha estimates one at a time.

A model can therefore have:

  • high $R^2$;
  • relatively small mean absolute alpha;
  • only a handful of individually significant alphas;

and still fail the GRS test because the portfolio pricing errors are jointly inconsistent with zero.

For the common sample:

  • $T=756$ monthly observations;
  • $N=25$ test portfolios;
  • $K=1$ for CAPM;
  • $K=3$ for FF3;
  • $K=5$ for FF5.

Rolling Factor Premia

The rolling 12-month factor return compounds the preceding 12 monthly returns:

$$R_t^{(12)}=\prod_{j=0}^{11}(1+R_{t-j})-1$$

Figure 4. Rolling 12-month cumulative returns for the Fama-French factors.

The rolling-return analysis provides a descriptive view of how factor realizations vary through time. It is not used as a separate asset-pricing test.

Distributional Diagnostics

Portfolio excess returns display positive skewness and substantial excess kurtosis, particularly among small portfolios.

For Small/Low-B/M:

  • excess kurtosis ≈ 33.35

Across all 25 portfolios:

These diagnostics show that the portfolio-return distributions are far from Gaussian in several dimensions.

Figure 5. Correlation matrix of excess returns across the 25 Size/Book-to-Market portfolios.

Beta vs. Return

This is a descriptive diagnostic rather than a formal CAPM test.

Market beta is defined as:

$$\beta_i=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_i-R_f,\;Mkt-RF)}{\operatorname{Var}(Mkt-RF)}$$

Figure 6. Average portfolio excess returns plotted against market beta.

The cross-sectional beta–return relationship is weak, providing descriptive evidence against a simple one-factor explanation and motivating the formal CAPM analysis in Notebook 04.

Model

The CAPM regression is:

$$R_{i,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha_i+\beta_i(Mkt-RF)_t+\varepsilon_{i,t}$$

The pricing restriction is:

$$H_0:\alpha_i=0$$

for every portfolio.

OLS with Newey-West HAC (12-lag) inference is used throughout.

FF5 Verdict

FF5 provides the strongest overall statistical fit, but its incremental improvement over FF3 is limited:

  • Mean absolute alpha is essentially unchanged.
  • Mean $R^2$ increases by only 0.6 percentage points.
  • Mean residual volatility falls only modestly.
  • BH-significant alphas increase from 3 to 4.
  • The GRS statistic improves, but the model remains decisively rejected.

FF5 improves explanatory power and residual risk modestly, but does not fundamentally change the pricing result established by FF3.

The evidence strongly favors moving beyond the CAPM for this portfolio set.

The largest improvement occurs when moving from CAPM to FF3. Mean absolute alpha falls from 0.198% to 0.087%, mean $R^2$ rises from 73.42% to 91.00%, and mean residual volatility falls from 2.94% to 1.66%. FF3 also produces the smallest absolute alpha for 13 of the 25 portfolios, indicating that its improvement is broad-based rather than concentrated in only a small subset of assets.

Adding profitability and investment through FF5 produces a much smaller incremental improvement. Mean $R^2$ rises by only 0.64 percentage points, mean residual volatility falls by only 0.06 percentage points, and mean absolute alpha remains effectively unchanged at 0.087%. FF5 does achieve the highest $R^2$, lowest residual volatility, and lowest GRS statistic, but these gains do not translate into a general elimination of pricing errors.

The individual-alpha evidence is also important. FF3 leaves 3 BH-significant alphas, while FF5 leaves 4. None of the three FF3-surviving significant alphas disappears after adding RMW and CMA; instead, one additional portfolio becomes significant under FF5. This suggests that the incremental contribution of the additional factors is selective rather than uniformly corrective.

Most importantly, none of the three models passes the GRS test:

ModelGRSDecision
CAPM4.170Rejected
FF33.610Rejected
FF53.227Rejected

The GRS statistic declines monotonically as factors are added, but the joint zero-alpha restriction remains decisively rejected even under the five-factor specification.

Bottom Line

FF3 is the strongest pragmatic and parsimonious benchmark for this portfolio set.

It captures almost all of the improvement available beyond CAPM while using only two additional factors.

FF5 is a reasonable expanded benchmark, particularly when maximizing time-series fit is the objective, but its incremental contribution beyond FF3 is small and concentrated rather than broad-based.

The broader lesson is:

Better explanatory fit does not necessarily mean complete pricing success.

All three models improve on the previous specification as factors are added, but none fully explains the joint cross-section of these 25 test portfolios.

Limitations

Several limitations define how these results should be interpreted.

Test-asset choice

The analysis uses the 25 Size × Book-to-Market portfolios rather than individual securities or a broader cross-section of test assets.

The conclusions therefore describe model performance for this particular portfolio set.

Sample dependence

The head-to-head comparison uses the July 1963 – June 2026 common sample.

Results may differ over another historical period.

Inference

Newey-West HAC standard errors with 12 lags account for heteroskedasticity and serial correlation in the regression residuals, but they do not resolve every possible issue surrounding statistical inference in asset-pricing tests.

Multiple testing

Benjamini-Hochberg correction controls the false-discovery rate for the 25 individual alpha tests. It is a multiple-testing adjustment for the individual hypotheses and is distinct from the joint GRS test.

GRS interpretation

GRS rejection shows that the model does not jointly price this particular set of 25 portfolios.

It does not imply that the model is useless for every possible asset or portfolio.

Descriptive vs. structural interpretation

The analysis evaluates the empirical pricing performance of the factor models.

It does not establish a causal economic mechanism explaining why the factors earn returns.

Model comparison

The FF3 and FF5 models contain more factors than CAPM, so improvements in fit are expected mechanically to some extent. The comparison therefore places particular emphasis on the magnitude of incremental gains rather than simply asking whether fit improves.

Final Takeaway

CAPM is not enough for these 25 portfolios. FF3 produces the dominant improvement. FF5 improves fit further, but only marginally, and none of the models passes the joint GRS test.

The empirical comparison therefore points to a clear conclusion:

$$\boxed{\text{CAPM} \;\rightarrow\; \textbf{FF3} \;\rightarrow\; \text{FF5}}$$

with the CAPM → FF3 transition providing most of the economic and statistical improvement, while the FF3 → FF5 transition provides only a modest incremental gain.
